Crisis / care / making cures

When a world stops holding.

What happens when familiar ways of living, thinking, and caring no longer hold? Follow Heidegger’s crisis, the encounters between philosophy and psychotherapy, and experiments in making more livable worlds together.

Breakdown is a field of inquiry across biography, institutions, and practices. A failed tool, an unsettled understanding, and a person’s suffering require different descriptions. None guarantees revelation or liberation.

Heidegger’s crisis: receiving help.

Historical scholarship Andrew J. Mitchell’s “Heidegger’s Breakdown” reconstructs Heidegger’s postwar crisis and treatment by Viktor Emil von Gebsattel. The treatment belongs to 1946; retrospective accounts and the sequence of denazification proceedings need careful comparison. Gebsattel, rather than Medard Boss, occupies the central clinical role in this episode.

Mitchell reads the encounter alongside changing accounts of health, help, and healing. Heidegger’s recollection emphasizes walking with Gebsattel and the help of another human being. Mitchell questions the claim that the doctor simply did nothing: he also documents continuing psychotherapy and glucose injections. The walking narrative is not a complete treatment history. Restraint and presence matter here alongside evidence of active treatment.

Keep Heidegger’s account, the biographical evidence, and Mitchell’s philosophical interpretation distinct. His suffering does not cancel political responsibility for his National Socialist commitments; neither does it authorize a diagnosis from a distance.

Mitchell, Research in Phenomenology 46 (2016), 70–97; especially 80–87

Boss / Zollikon: philosophy enters the consulting room.

Primary collection Heidegger’s correspondence with the Swiss psychiatrist Medard Boss began in 1947. Their subsequent collaboration produced the Zollikon seminars, held across 1959–1969. The published collection combines seminar protocols, conversations, and letters; these genres do not provide interchangeable kinds of evidence.

Read for questions of bodily existence, space, time, causality, and being with others. How does describing someone as an object of measurement differ from encountering a person in a world? Then turn the question back on the encounter: who decides what the patient’s world means?

The ontological analysis of Dasein and clinical Daseinsanalysis operate at different levels. Boss and Ludwig Binswanger also developed different projects. “Existential psychotherapy” should not erase those disagreements.

A challenge from within Anthony Stadlen’s The Madhouse of Being asks how language can darken and mystify a shared world. Read his scrutiny of Zollikon’s clinical discussions as a challenge to philosophical and therapeutic authority, not as an endorsement of every claim in that tradition.

Foucault: existential analysis and its changing stakes.

Documented connection Foucault’s early route runs through Binswanger: a 1953 course and his introduction to the 1954 French translation of Dream and Existence. His early manuscript, now published as Binswanger and Existential Analysis, compares existential analysis with psychiatry, psychoanalysis, and phenomenology.

This is a separate route from Boss’s Zollikon collaboration. Foucault’s later histories of madness and psychiatric power change the questions: how do institutions and forms of knowledge establish who may speak, what counts as truth, and what treatment authorizes?

His preface to Anti-Oedipus gives another concrete crossing to Deleuze and Guattari. These encounters do not establish one continuous therapeutic school.

Different approaches. Questions to keep open.

BeyngLab comparison Use these questions to enter the texts. They are neither a ranking of treatments nor complete definitions of internally diverse traditions.

The researcher enters the picture.

Research method In The Wounded Researcher, Robert D. Romanyshyn asks how the researcher distinguishes personal investments in a topic from what the work asks of them. His account develops transference dialogues, active imagination, and alchemical hermeneutics. Begin with the preface, chapters 5–6, and chapters 10–11.

For BeyngLab this opens a question about our own inquiry: what do we hope a story of breakdown will prove? Keep a record of attachments, uncertainties, and counter-readings. A wound may help explain why a question matters; it does not settle the answer.

Cross to the Imaginal grammar while preserving the difference between Romanyshyn’s Jungian approach and other accounts of imagination. Regional disclosure grammars remain revisable descriptions of practices, never fixed cultural essences.

Sixfold & Making Cures: make with.

StudioLab / BeyngLab “Making cures” supplements talking cures and taking cures through creative doing-with oneself, others, and an environment. Media making, playform building, and cosmography become ways to work on shared conditions of life. The term’s perfumative register also lets us think of curing through time, materials, atmosphere, and relation.

BREAKDOWN

Notice what no longer holds.

Whose account describes the trouble? Separate a person’s distress from a failed arrangement, a broken tool, or a disputed expectation.

SIXFOLD

Map the gathering.

Read earth, sky, mortals, and divinities; make Thing and World explicit. What gathers here, and what wider conditions support or constrain it?

MAKING CURES

Change something together.

Make a small, revisable intervention. Return it to those involved, attend to its effects, and keep open the possibility of stopping.

These moves can recur in any order. Sixfold is a BeyngLab cosmographic exercise, not a diagnostic instrument. Making cures names a practice of collaborative inquiry and transformation, not a promise of clinical recovery.

Begin with a shared table, meeting, classroom, or workplace. A story, rearranged space, changed schedule, or agreed pause may matter more than another product. Rest, refusal, and collaborative not-doing belong here: care must not become a fresh demand to perform.
