The Wiseacre at Law

Anarchy, Just Gaming, and the Performance of Jurisprudence

Abstract

Doug Wise’s essay “Two Hermeneutical Motives in Reading the Constitution” translates the American dispute between textualism and pragmatism into a conflict between automaticity and plasticity. Read by itself, this translation risks turning jurisprudential disagreement into a choice between stable rules and adaptive judgment. Read alongside Reiner Schürmann’s account of the tragic double bind and Jean-François Lyotard’s account of judging without criteria, however, Wise’s intervention opens a more radical question: how can law establish a common world while remaining answerable to singular claims that its categories cannot recognize? This essay argues that jurisprudence operates under two irreducible demands. Law must contextualize, classify, and stabilize; justice must also expose every context, classification, and settlement to interruption. Schürmann names this conflict an-archic because no higher principle can reconcile its terms. Lyotard names its practice just gaming: judgment case by case without a final metalanguage. To clarify what follows for jurisprudence, the essay develops three figures: wisdom inhabits the double bind, wisedumb converts it into a single rule, and the wiseacre interrupts every rule that mistakes its historical authority for the order of being. Law, on this account, does not merely regulate a world. It performs one.

Keywords: jurisprudence; Heidegger; Doug Wise; Reiner Schürmann; Jean-François Lyotard; anarchy; double bind; textualism; pragmatism; performance

The Jurisprudential Wiseacre

Doug Wise does not approach Heidegger as the custodian of a finished doctrine. His essays carry Heideggerian concepts into evolutionary biology, thermodynamics, topology, semiotics, film, constitutional interpretation, and other domains that professional Heidegger scholarship often treats as external applications. This practice does not simply illustrate Heidegger. It places Heideggerian thinking at risk. A concept must work outside the protected climate of its commentary, or its failure must teach us something.

The name for this practice is wiseacring. The wiseacre interrupts respectable intelligence with an impertinent question. He notices where a system’s official explanation ceases to describe what the system actually does. He treats disciplinary boundaries less as prohibitions than as invitations to trespass. The wiseacre may get the joke wrong, force an analogy, or press a concept past its warranted range. Yet these dangers belong to the experiment. Without such risk, wisdom congeals into the recitation of wisdom.

Wise’s “Two Hermeneutical Motives in Reading the Constitution” offers an unusually concrete example.1

2 Its subject, however, is not simply which interpretive method judges should adopt. Wise asks what kind of world must already have disclosed itself for textualism or pragmatism to appear compelling. “Tell me what is your stance on interpreting,” his essay begins, “and I’ll tell you who you are.” The sentence relocates jurisprudence. A theory of interpretation becomes a way of existing in a legal world.

Wise begins with John Manning’s influential account of the difference between textualism and purposivism. Textualists privilege semantic context: how a reasonable reader, conversant with the relevant conventions, would understand the enacted language. Purposivists allow evidence of policy purpose to overcome even strong semantic cues when a literal reading would frustrate the mischief the law addresses.3

Manning defends textualism as the method most respectful of legislative supremacy and compromise. The words that survive the legislative process mark the bargain that achieved enactment; judges who relax their semantic constraints may replace that bargain with their reconstruction of what the legislature ought to have wanted.

Wise redescribes this controversy through David Bates’s polarity of automaticity and plasticity. Textualism values the reliable execution of instructions. It seeks rules judges can apply repeatedly, publicly, and with limited discretion. In Wise’s cybernetic language, the judge becomes a dependable component or servo-mechanism within a constitutional system. Pragmatism instead emphasizes adjustment. Because technologies, institutions, habits, and meanings change, interpretation must remain capable of modifying its operations. It must assess purposes and consequences, learn from feedback, and sometimes break with inherited instructions.

The pairing is illuminating because it reveals the temporal commitments hidden inside interpretive methods. Textualism protects continuity against the retrospective invention of purposes. Pragmatism protects responsiveness against the mechanical survival of rules whose worlds have disappeared. But Wise’s pairing also raises a problem. If jurisprudence becomes a choice between automaticity and plasticity, then one might imagine an optimal setting somewhere between them: enough rule for predictability, enough flexibility for change. The conflict would become an engineering problem. The system would require calibration rather than judgment.

Wise himself prevents that easy solution. He suggests that textualism and pragmatism express different Grundbefindlichkeiten, fundamental ways of finding oneself in and being affected by a world. The interpreter does not first encounter neutral legal materials and then select a method. What counts as fidelity, danger, arbitrariness, democratic legitimacy, or institutional survival has already acquired significance. Beneath the published rationale lie what Benjamin Cardozo called the forces that make the judge, and what Heidegger calls Befindlichkeit: the always already affective disclosure of a situation.4 This suggestion should not be mistaken for a causal psychology of judges. Wise has not established that textualists possess one temperament and pragmatists another, nor would such a diagnosis decide the legal merits of either position. His intervention works at another level. It asks jurisprudence to account for the world-disclosing commitments its technical arguments presuppose. That question leads from Wise to Schürmann.

From Automaticity and Plasticity to the Double Bind

Reiner Schürmann’s work changes the structure of the dispute. In Heidegger on Being and Acting, he asks what becomes of action once thinking can no longer provide it with a secure first principle. Western practical philosophy repeatedly derives action from an archē: an origin that also commands, a foundation that also rules. A highest good, a divine order, rational nature, autonomous subjectivity, historical progress, or another hegemonic principle supplies both the intelligibility of a world and the norm by which conduct within it is measured. Schürmann calls action an-archic when the authority of such ultimate referents withers.5

An-archy does not mean that practices suddenly proceed without rules, institutions, precedents, or obligations. It names the loss of a final appellate authority capable of legitimating them all. Principles remain operative but lose their claim to stand outside the histories and conflicts they order. They become finite settlements. Anarchy is therefore not a new sovereign principle announcing that no principles are permitted. Such a prohibition would merely enthrone another archē. It is exposure to the absence of final ground.

Schürmann’s later work makes this exposure tragic. “Tragedy consists in a legal double bind,” he writes in “Ultimate Double Binds.”6 Antigone and Creon owe allegiance to incompatible laws: the customs of familial burial and the edicts of the city. Each chooses one law and denies the other. Their hubris lies not only in selecting the wrong norm but in converting an irreducible conflict into a single bind. Neither law can absorb the other; no higher law reconciles them without remainder.

Schürmann generalizes this tragic structure beyond the collision of civic and familial obligations. Being binds phenomena according to two incompatible laws. Under the law of phenomenalization or contextualization, a thing enters a world. It becomes recognizable through common names, practices, categories, histories, and relations. Under the law of singularization or decontextualization, the thing exceeds the context that makes it appear. It withdraws from substitution, interrupts classification, or becomes expelled from the world that had rendered it intelligible. Heidegger’s appropriation and expropriation name this strife without synthesizing it.7

The juridical implications are immediate. Law must phenomenalize. It must identify persons and offices, distinguish permissions from injuries, assign jurisdictions, establish procedures, preserve precedents, and render actions publicly intelligible. The common world depends on such contextualization. A legal order incapable of repeating categories could not make promises, coordinate conduct, or constrain power.

Yet the singular case is never only an instance of its category. A person appears before the law through legally legible predicates, but no collection of predicates exhausts that person. A harm may occur for which the governing law possesses no idiom. A new technology may reorganize agency before doctrine knows how to assign responsibility. The violence does not lie only in a bad classification. Schürmann calls the subsumption of singulars under an ultimate universal an arch-violence. Every hegemonic world converts what happens once into an example of what may happen repeatedly. Such conversion is unavoidable: without common terms, singulars could not enter shared intelligibility at all. But the conversion becomes hubristic when the common term forgets the singular remainder it cannot contain.8

Wise’s automaticity and plasticity can now be read as the institutional surface of Schürmann’s deeper conflict. Automaticity belongs to law’s need for repeatable phenomenalization. Plasticity belongs to its exposure to singularizing interruption. The two are not adjustable values on one scale. Each names a demand the other cannot satisfy. A perfectly automatic legal order extinguishes the singular under the rule. A perfectly plastic order destroys the common space in which a claim can receive legal recognition. Jurisprudence does not solve this conflict. It inhabits it.

Schürmann’s later language also prevents an overly cheerful celebration of difference. Broken Hegemonies describes human existence as pulled between natality and mortality, universalization and singularization. Natality begins, institutes, and gathers; mortality withdraws, disperses, and renders every beginning finite. Neither side can become a program. The appeal of principles clashes with the appeal of singularization, depriving us of a simple court of appeal. “The origin thus proves to be anarchic,” Schürmann concludes, “because in dissension with itself.”9 The formula matters for any project that pluralizes worlds or Beyngs. Pluralization can secretly install a higher unity from which the plurality is surveyed. Regional worlds become specimens inside a universal atlas, and the atlas becomes the unspoken sovereign. Schürmann offers no metalanguage above the regions. He locates dissension at the origin itself. The one does not first exist and subsequently diversify; neither does multiplicity rest upon a more foundational multiplicity. What binds is already double.

Lyotard and the Play of Justice

If Schürmann identifies the bind, Jean-François Lyotard asks how judgment remains possible inside it. Just Gaming, the conversation between Lyotard and Jean-Loup Thébaud published in French as Au juste, begins after confidence in universal criteria has broken down. No ontology, philosophy of history, science of society, or revolutionary metanarrative can derive prescriptive justice from descriptive truth. One cannot move from what is to what ought to be by applying a theoretical bridge.

Lyotard’s answer is deliberately severe: “we judge without criteria.”10 The absence of ultimate criteria does not excuse judgment; it makes judgment unavoidable. If a rule determined the result in advance, no judgment worthy of the name would occur. Responsibility begins where deduction ends. The word gaming easily invites a mistake. Lyotard does not trivialize justice as playacting, nor does he reduce politics to a contest in which any clever move counts. Language games possess rules. distribute the positions of sender, addressee, referent, and sense; they establish which phrases count as evidence, command, testimony, knowledge, or obligation. No game exists without constraints. But no rule governs all games or dictates how heterogeneous games must relate.

The judge therefore proceeds case by case, closer to Aristotelian phronēsis than to the application of a universal algorithm. Yet Lyotard removes Aristotle’s shared civic telos. Practical wisdom can no longer presume agreement about the human good that judgments ought to realize. It must invent criteria in response to a case while knowing that those criteria cannot retroactively acquire transcendent authority.11

This account becomes politically sharper in The Differend. A litigation occurs when parties can state their claims within a shared idiom and submit them to an accepted rule of judgment. A differend arises when the idiom in which judgment occurs deprives one party of the means to formulate or prove the wrong. The victim suffers both a damage and the loss of the language in which the damage could count.12 Justice cannot consist simply in neutrally applying the governing rule, because the neutrality of that rule constitutes the wrong.

Lyotard therefore defines injustice at a level prior to incorrect verdicts. Injustice occurs when the possibility of continuing to play the game of the just is prohibited. Terror does not merely make an unjust move; it monopolizes the field, silences competing phrases, and declares the question closed. Justice preserves the possibility that another claim, idiom, or rule may become necessary. This is not pluralism as equal hospitality to every discourse. Some language games exist by rendering others silent. A legal order that recognizes testimony only in the form its violence has made impossible does not deserve protection as one game among many. Keeping justice in play may require dismantling the rule that blocks the victim’s phrase. Lyotard’s plurality therefore does not collapse into the marketplace fantasy that all positions compete on equal terms.

Schürmann explicitly cites Lyotard’s Differend when describing the irreconcilable conflict between phenomenalization and singularization. The relation is precise. Phenomenalization supplies the idioms through which a case becomes litigable. Singularization marks what no established idiom can phrase without remainder. A differend is the juridico-linguistic trace of the ontological double bind. Wise now appears differently as well. Textualism is not unjust simply because it follows rules, and pragmatism is not just simply because it adapts them. The decisive question is what each method allows to become a legal phrase. Textualism may protect marginalized parties against judges who redescribe enacted compromises according to elite preferences. Pragmatism may hear an injury that inherited semantics exclude. Either method can open a claim; either can silence one. Justice cannot be assigned in advance to one jurisprudential camp.

Clearing and Enframing Wise gives this double bind an arresting constitutional formulation. Drawing on Breyer’s description of the United States as an experiment, he proposes that the Constitution can be understood as a political and legal Lichtung: a clearing in which the experiment of collective existence proceeds. Constitutional limits can prevent an official authority from prescribing one orthodox account of politics, religion, nationalism, or human meaning. Law then preserves a space in which heterogeneous forms of life can appear.

Wise Heideggerizes Chief Justice John Marshall’s famous admonition: “We must never forget that it is a Lichtung we are expounding.”13 The sentence does not claim that Marshall secretly anticipated Heidegger or that constitutional doctrine follows from fundamental ontology. It is an experimental translation. The Constitution appears not only as a superior text but as a historically instituted opening that organizes the appearance of public actors and claims.

Wise immediately supplies the counter-formula: “We must never forget that it is a Ge-Stell we are expounding.”14 The same Constitution that opens a political world assigns competencies, orders territories, classifies persons, authorizes coercion, and converts conflicts into administrable forms. It calls beings to present themselves in ways the legal apparatus can process. Clearing and enframing do not describe two separate constitutions, one emancipatory and the other oppressive. They name incompatible traits of the same juridical performance.

This pairing should not become a loose metaphor in which Lichtung means anything good and Ge-Stell anything bad. A clearing excludes as it opens. Enframing can secure rights through stable procedures as well as reduce beings to resources. The force of Wise’s juxtaposition lies precisely in its refusal of purity. The law’s world-opening power and its ordering violence arrive together.

Schürmann allows us to sharpen the point. Constitutional law phenomenalizes a polity: it lets offices, powers, citizens, rights, and disputes appear in a shared order. At the same time, singularization works within that order as an undertow. Persons and events exceed the identities through which the Constitution recognizes them. Constitutional judgment responds to this undertow by revising a category, inventing a new idiom, or refusing the claim. No amendment, precedent, or doctrine eliminates the conflict. Each gives it another historical form.

Lyotard adds a criterion that is not a final criterion: ask whether the judgment keeps the question of justice open. Does the decision permit the excluded claim to become phraseable? Does it acknowledge a differend, or does it disguise one as a routine litigation? Does a rule coordinate a plurality of actors, or does it secure its apparent universality by making some injuries legally inaudible? These questions do not generate an algorithm. They test whether the game remains a game or has become terror.

Wisdom Wisedumb and the Wiseacre

The conjunction of Wise, Schürmann, and Lyotard permits three new terms. They should not be treated as synonyms for intelligence, stupidity, and mockery. Each names a relation to the double bind. Wisdom inhabits the double bind without promising to overcome it. It recognizes that law needs repetition and interruption, common categories and singular claims, institutional memory and transformative judgment. Wisdom does not locate justice at a stable midpoint. It accepts that each decision exposes the judge to incompatible demands and that no method removes responsibility for the cut a decision makes.

Wisedumb is wisdom after it has forgotten the event that once made it wise. A distinction, maxim, precedent, or institutional safeguard survives its encounter with a singular problem and begins to circulate as a self-justifying answer. Wisedumb is therefore not the absence of knowledge. It is congealed knowledge, armored competence, the automatic recitation of solutions detached from the conditions that gave them sense.

Wisedumb assumes at least two forms. Archic wisedumb insists that the rule has already decided, leaving the interpreter only to execute it. Anarchistic wisedumb announces that, because no rule possesses ultimate authority, every decision amounts to an equally legitimate invention. The first sacrifices singularization to order. The second sacrifices the common world to discretionary power. Both escape the double bind by absolutizing one of its demands.

The wiseacre prevents that escape. The wiseacre is wisdom’s immanent heckler. When textualism presents itself as a neutral machine, the wiseacre exposes the histories, affects, and political settlements sedimented in its semantics. When pragmatism congratulates itself on flexibility, the wiseacre asks who controls adaptation, whose consequences count, and which managerial system defines successful feedback. When anarchy becomes an identity or program, the wiseacre catches the new principle putting on the mask of nonprinciple.

The wiseacre consequently practices a comic form of Schürmannian tragic knowledge. Tragedy reveals that the two laws cannot be reconciled. Comedy catches each law impersonating the whole. Its switcheroo suddenly changes the as: the faithful servant appears as sovereign, the neutral procedure as political settlement, the emancipatory innovation as a new mechanism of control. The joke does not dissolve the conflict. It interrupts the solemnity by which one term claims exclusive jurisdiction. This figure also clarifies Wise’s place within Heidegger studies. He does not provide the definitive interpretation of Heidegger, nor do his scientific and juridical conjunctions enjoy automatic authority because they are inventive. His value lies in putting canonical distinctions under foreign pressure. Wiseacring becomes a method of experimental scholarship: cite precisely, translate boldly, distinguish analogy from evidence, and leave the seam visible where interpretation becomes construction.

Law Performs a World

The synthesis advanced here belongs neither to Wise, Schürmann, nor Lyotard alone. It is a BeyngLab extension: law performs a world. Performance in this formulation means more than theatrical representation and more than the linguistic performative that accomplishes an act by being uttered under authorized conditions. It names the iterative composition of a field in which beings, actions, and relations acquire significance.

Law performs persons by instituting capacities, liabilities, statuses, and standing. It performs things by distinguishing property, evidence, resource, weapon, body, border, corporation, environment, and data. It performs time through limitation periods, precedents, emergencies, terms of office, inheritance, and the retroactive or prospective force of decisions. It performs space through jurisdiction, sovereignty, zoning, enclosure, migration, and the distribution of public and private domains. None of these performances creates its material ex nihilo. Each selects, stabilizes, and enforces ways in which beings may matter.

This account does not reduce law to discourse. A judgment worlds through police, prisons, databases, deeds, contracts, architectures, bureaucratic routines, screens, and habits of anticipation. Juridical performance recruits bodies and technologies. It does not merely say what the world is; it builds interfaces through which the world becomes actionable.

Wise’s automaticity names the iterative side of this performance. Legal forms must be repeatable to become institutions. Schürmann shows why no repetition can fully master singularization. Lyotard shows why judgment must sometimes invent a phrase or rule that the existing game does not authorize. Performance holds these together: sedimentation makes a world; variation changes what can appear within it.

The argument also bears on regional Beyngs. Different legal orders do not simply apply different positive rules to an identical inventory of beings. They may disclose different persons, collectivities,

lands, ancestors, obligations, injuries, and temporalities. A river may appear as property, navigable water, sacred ancestor, legal person, infrastructure, or living relation. These are not merely competing descriptions laid over an unchanged object. Each belongs to a material-juridical performance that organizes who can act, what can be claimed, and how damage can be heard.

Yet pluralizing such worlds risks projecting a higher world that contains and adjudicates them all. Schürmann and Lyotard help resist that move. There is no neutral super-region from which every regional Beyng can be translated without loss. Translation itself occurs under a double bind. A common idiom is necessary for adjudication, while the common idiom threatens to erase the singularity of what it translates. The task is not to escape this trap by discovering the correct metalanguage. It is to construct institutions capable of registering the loss, contesting their own categories, and inventing new phrases when a differend becomes perceptible.

Objections and Limits

Four cautions follow from the argument.

First, ontology cannot decide doctrine. Heidegger’s account of disclosure does not tell a court how to interpret a statute, and Schürmann’s double bind does not determine the outcome of a constitutional case. Ontological analysis may expose what a jurisprudential method presupposes or excludes; it cannot substitute for historical, institutional, and doctrinal argument.

Second, Wise’s alignment of textualism with automaticity and pragmatism with plasticity remains interpretive. Textualist practice contains judgments about context, genre, precedent, and linguistic convention; pragmatism depends upon stabilized rules and institutional competence. The polarity identifies tendencies, not pure camps. Used dogmatically, it would reproduce the wisedumb it seeks to diagnose.

Third, judging without ultimate criteria does not mean judging without reasons. Lyotard’s argument intensifies the obligation to articulate reasons because no foundation guarantees them. A judgment must expose itself to other phrases, histories, and consequences. The absence of a final criterion does not equal the absence of better and worse judgments.

Fourth, keeping the game open cannot by itself tell us which institutions can resist domination. A powerful actor may celebrate plural games while controlling the material conditions of play. Juridical performance therefore requires political economy: attention to who owns the platforms, controls enforcement, funds litigation, defines evidence, and survives delay. Lyotard’s injustice of silencing must be traced through the machinery that produces silence.

These limits do not weaken the synthesis. They locate its proper work. Wise, Schürmann, and Lyotard do not give jurisprudence another comprehensive theory. They disclose why comprehensive theories repeatedly become dangerous: each is tempted to convert one binding demand into the sole law of the whole.

Conclusion

Wise begins from an argument between textualism and pragmatism and discovers two hermeneutical worlds. Schürmann deepens the argument by showing that the opposition expresses an originary legal double bind: phenomena must enter common contexts, yet singularization withdraws them from every final context. Lyotard supplies the practice demanded by this an-archic condition. We judge without a metalanguage, case by case, responsible for maintaining the possibility that another claim can be phrased.

The resulting jurisprudence neither worships rules nor romanticizes their disruption. Law must stabilize a world, but justice keeps that stabilization from becoming the last word. Wisdom inhabits the conflict. Wisedumb denies it by turning either repetition or innovation into a sovereign principle. The wiseacre interrupts that sovereignty, exposing where a rule mistakes its historical victory for ontological necessity.

Law therefore performs a world by stabilizing some differences, authorizing some interpretations, and suppressing others. Justice begins when a singular claim reveals what this performance cannot hear. No higher principle resolves the encounter. The responsibility to judge remains.

For BeyngLab, this is not only an interpretation of jurisprudence. It is a protocol for thinking. Preserve the distinction between primary text, editorial mediation, historical evidence, later interpretation, and experimental extension. Then permit the levels to encounter one another without pretending they have merged. The seam is not a defect. It is where thinking begins.
