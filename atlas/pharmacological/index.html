A5 / ATLAS GRAMMAR

A revisable operation of disclosure

Pharmacological

What does a technique enable, and what care does it put at risk?

For Bernard Stiegler, technical supports help form memory, attention, and shared life. Their effects call for a pharmacological critique: the same arrangement may support a capacity and undermine it. BeyngLab uses this lens on one film situation, not as a diagnosis of a people, a device, or an entire culture.

01 / Operation

Follow the support and its effects.

A pharmakon can carry beneficial and harmful possibilities. Stiegler’s analysis of digital relational technologies puts attention and care at stake; these steps are a BeyngLab protocol for one situated encounter.[1][7]

  1. 01

    Locate the support

    Name the tools, recorded traces, skills, institutions, and people that make this situation possible. Ask who makes and maintains the support.

  2. 02

    Trace the double effect

    Which forms of memory, attention, or coordination become possible? Which are shortened, captured, or displaced? The two effects need not balance neatly.[1]

  3. 03

    Try a counter-practice

    Ask what participants could change in the use or governance of the support, and who should judge whether that change improves care.[1]

02 / Philosophical hinge

Keep the questions distinct.

Heidegger

In “The Question Concerning Technology,” enframing names a way in which beings are revealed as orderable standing-reserve. This is a question about revealing, not a claim that a phone or camera is itself enframing.[2]

Stiegler

In “Relational Ecology and the Digital Pharmakon,” Stiegler describes attention as formed through social life and material techniques. He asks how externalized memory and digital relational technologies might capture attention or sustain critical participation.[1]

Cinema and this site

Stiegler makes cinematic time a subject of a separate book. The present film exercise extends questions from his essay to an analogue camera and a mobile phone in Kiarostami’s fiction. It is BeyngLab’s comparison, not a reading Stiegler made of this film.[3]

Boundary. This lens travels across situations. It cannot identify a “technological culture” or rank places by their media. The film pairing is a test of what the questions disclose and conceal, not a history of influence.[7]

03 / Worked encounter · plot details

A phone call above the village

Abbas Kiarostami, The Wind Will Carry Us, 1999 · hilltop return

A crew has come to a Kurdish village to film a funeral rite while an elderly woman remains alive. The visiting filmmaker repeatedly goes uphill for mobile reception, near a worker digging out of sight. Choose one such return for a close reading; this page gives no edition-dependent timecode.[4][6]

What is recorded
The phone directs the visitor uphill and connects him to people elsewhere. The planned camera work gives the crew a reason to wait for a death. The digger’s work and voice persist outside the image. Begin with the particular shot and sound you can verify.
What the support affords
A call can coordinate work at a distance; a camera can preserve and circulate an event. The film itself uses cinematic means to let viewers notice the crew’s impatience. These are possible capacities, not a verdict on every use.
What the arrangement costs
A production schedule turns the woman’s continued life into a delay for the visitor. The calls repeatedly reorient him toward demands elsewhere. Akbar reads the audience’s wish to picture unseen lives as part of this ethical problem.
What resists the diagnosis
Brody also reads the film’s means of communication and cinema as possible openings for contact and freedom. The digger’s work, the woman’s life, and local relationships exceed the crew’s plan. Do not make the phone the sole cause of the harm.
Revised claim
This scene lets several times meet uneasily: a remote deadline, a worksite, a film crew’s anticipation, and a woman’s living time. Test how the phone and camera mediate those relations without turning technics into the whole explanation.

Experimental The film analysis draws on Akbar’s and Brody’s differing readings[5][6]. Stiegler did not make this reading of Kiarostami. Compare it with the Imaginal lens ↗

04 / Capture and correction

Where this reading can go wrong.

Capture riskWhat happensCounter-practice
Device scapegoatThe phone becomes the villain; the crew’s purpose, employer, and relation to villagers disappear.Describe the entire arrangement and ask which decision or demand directs the device.
Managed careA proposed remedy turns attention into a score assigned by an expert.Let the people affected contest both the diagnosis and the proposed change.
False equivalenceHeidegger’s enframing, Stiegler’s pharmacology, and Kiarostami’s film become one doctrine.Keep each claim beside its source and label the film comparison Experimental.

05 / Try and revise

Read a technique with its people.

Try these four moves with any well-described situation. For the film, use the guided two-lens exercise.

  1. SupportList the device, records, institutions, labor, and dependencies in one encounter.
  2. Double effectWrite one affordance and one cost, with a specific observation for each.
  3. ObjectionFind a use the diagnosis misses, or a person whose account is absent.
  4. RevisionChange the claim and propose a counter-practice someone could actually contest.

06 / Source ledger

What supports which claim.

  1. Primary
    Bernard Stiegler, “Relational Ecology and the Digital Pharmakon,” trans. Patrick Crogan, Culture Machine 13 (2012).

    Stiegler on attention, externalized memory, the pharmakon, and critical uses of digital relational technologies. The essay does not analyze Kiarostami.

  2. Primary
    Martin Heidegger, “The Question Concerning Technology,” in Basic Writings, Ereignis text archive.

    Enframing and standing-reserve as a mode of revealing. The film comparison does not attribute this vocabulary to Kiarostami.

  3. Historical
    Bernard Stiegler, Technics and Time, 3: Cinematic Time and the Question of Malaise, trans. Stephen Barker (Stanford University Press, 2010), publisher record.

    Bibliographic context for Stiegler’s separate work on cinematic time; the linked record is not a substitute for a passage from the book.

  4. Primary
    Janus Films, The Wind Will Carry Us, film record (Abbas Kiarostami, Iran, 1999).

    Distributor record for the crew, village, elderly woman, and unseen figures; close readings should return to the film.

  5. Interpretive
    Kaveh Akbar, “The Wind Will Carry Us: Dust to Dust,” The Criterion Collection, May 13, 2025.

    Film-specific reading of offscreen lives, labor, and the ethical risk in the visitor’s and viewer’s gaze.

  6. Interpretive
    Richard Brody, “DVD of the Week: The Wind Will Carry Us,” The New Yorker, February 22, 2010.

    Identifies the hilltop phone calls and unseen digger; offers a different emphasis on the possibilities of cinema and communication.

  7. Experimental
    BeyngLab, AIR V Regional Daseins / Sufi Heidegger report and this Atlas comparison.

    The three-move operation, film pairing, capture risks, and revision exercise are BeyngLab proposals.

The primary texts, distributor record, critics’ readings, and BeyngLab experiment carry different kinds of authority. The film itself remains the work to inspect.
