A1 / ATLAS GRAMMAR

A revisable operation of disclosure

Imaginal

When does an image disclose a world—and who gets to say so?

Henry Corbin used “imaginal” for an order of reality with its own mode of perception in the traditions he studied. BeyngLab borrows a question about mediation. This grammar cannot identify a people, certify an experience, or turn every striking image into an instance of the imaginal.

01 / Operation

What this lens lets us ask.

The imaginal is Corbin’s claim, with its own religious and philosophical stakes. These three moves form a BeyngLab reading protocol. They do not establish that any particular artwork belongs to an imaginal realm.[1][4]

  1. 01

    Attend to form

    Describe the image, its material, setting, maker, and the way it reaches you before declaring what it means.

  2. 02

    Ask how it appears

    What kind of attention or interpretive practice lets this image matter? Corbin’s cognitive imagination is a specific philosophical claim, not a synonym for creativity.[1]

  3. 03

    Return to a world

    Name the people, histories, institutions, and disagreements that the reading depends on. Test whether the image opens more than your first account can hold.

02 / Historical hinge

Keep the routes distinct.

Reception

Corbin encountered Heidegger’s hermeneutics and completed a French translation of What Is Metaphysics? in the 1930s. His sustained work on Suhrawardī followed a distinct intellectual and textual path. A reception history traces these contacts without treating one tradition as the hidden content of another.[2]

Corbin’s claim

In “Mundus Imaginalis,” Corbin distinguishes the imaginal from the merely unreal or fanciful. He writes of a mode of perception corresponding to an order of reality, drawing on the “eighth clime” and visionary accounts. That ontological claim belongs to Corbin and the traditions as he interprets them.[1]

BeyngLab’s move

Here the transferable question is narrower: what does an image make perceptible in this encounter, by what mediation, and at what cost? This is an editorial comparison. It is not a practice of ta’wil, a theological proof, or a definition of Islamicate thought.[4]

Boundary. “Regional” identifies this lens’s way of disclosing, not a fixed trait of Persia, Islam, or anyone living there. Corbin’s own broad geography is a historical interpretation to examine, never a map of cultural essence.[2]

03 / Worked encounter · plot details

The mirror in Travelers

Bahrām Bayzāʾī, Musāfirān (Travelers), 1991 · reading with Mansoor Behnam

An heirloom mirror is carried toward a wedding; after a fatal accident, the mirror is missing. The grandmother holds to the promise of its arrival. Mansoor Behnam reads the film’s mirror and its return through hope, absence, presence, and imaginal mediation.[3]

What the film gives
A particular mirror, a disrupted journey, a family preparing a wedding and then a funeral, and a staging of reflected light. Begin with these choices of object, performance, and film form.
What the lens proposes
The mirror can be read as a threshold at which the absent remain active in the family’s world. This is Behnam’s scholarly interpretation and a BeyngLab comparison; neither establishes that Bayzāʾī adopted Corbin’s philosophy.
What pushes back
The accident, women’s actions, mourning practices, the material heirloom, and the film’s relation to taʿzīyah resist being dissolved into a free-floating spiritual symbol.
Revised claim
In this film, the mirror’s movement changes how kinship, death, and hope can appear together. Return to a specific sequence and let another reading alter that sentence.

Interpretive Read the film and Behnam’s argument together. This example tests the lens; it does not confer a “Sufi” identity on Bayzāʾī or his film.

04 / Film resonance · plot details

Kiarostami, at the edge of the frame

Abbas Kiarostami, The Wind Will Carry Us, 1999 · BeyngLab film resonance

A television crew travels from Tehran to a Kurdish village to film an anticipated funeral. The elderly woman remains alive and unseen. The crew waits; the film lets other lives and voices continue beyond its frame.[5]

What the film gives
A woman never shown, crew members heard but not seen, an offscreen digger, and a milkmaid at work in a dark barn. Delay frustrates the visitor’s timetable. Attend to framing, voices, labor, and the crew’s stated purpose before assigning a philosophical meaning.
What this lens asks
How does a life outside the frame organize what appears within it? This question about cinematic withholding is a BeyngLab experiment. It does not make an offscreen figure an instance of Corbin’s imaginal order.
What pushes back
Kaveh Akbar reads the audience’s imagining of unseen lives as part of the film’s ethical problem: the crew wants images of death, and viewers can presume to know people they cannot see. Withholding may mark a limit on the camera’s claim, rather than a hidden spiritual world.
Revised claim
The film’s unseen figures interrupt the visitor’s planned image of the village. Looking and listening become answerable to living people and their work, which do not fit his schedule. Test this claim against a particular sequence.

Experimental Akbar’s film reading sharpens the objection[6]; the comparison with Corbin is BeyngLab’s. Neither a documented influence nor a “Sufi filmmaker” identity is claimed. Compare a Pharmacological reading of the same film ↗

Where this reading can go wrong.

Capture riskWhat happensCounter-practice
Angelic abstractionA luminous reading erases bodies, labor, loss, or political conditions.Keep the material sequence and its social setting beside the symbolic reading.
Fixed cultural essence“Iranian” or “Sufi” becomes one timeless way of seeing, assigned to every work or person.Name a text, maker, scene, and interpreter; compare disagreements within the field.

06 / Try and revise

Take one image through four moves.

A ten-minute comparative exercise, not a reproduction of taʾwīl in a living tradition.

  1. ChooseBring an image or scene whose maker and context you can identify.
  2. DescribeRecord its form and conditions before proposing what world it opens.
  3. Counter-readAsk what it withholds and who would challenge your description.
  4. ReviseKeep the first claim, the objection, and the changed claim together.

07 / Source ledger

What supports which claim.

  1. Primary
    Henry Corbin, “Mundus Imaginalis, or the Imaginary and the Imaginal,” in Swedenborg and Esoteric Islam, trans. Leonard Fox (West Chester, PA: Swedenborg Foundation, 1995), online excerpt and continuations.

    Corbin’s distinction between imaginal and imaginary, and his account of cognitive imagination.

  2. Historical
    Daryush Shayegan, “Corbin, Henry,” Encyclopædia Iranica 6, no. 3 (1993): 268–72, updated February 2, 2015.

    Biographical and intellectual history of Corbin’s Heidegger encounter and Suhrawardī studies.

  3. Interpretive
    Mansoor Behnam, “Travelers (1991),” Cinema Iranica, May 21, 2025.

    A film-specific reading of the mirror, mourning, women’s roles, and imaginal mediation; it is not evidence of direct Corbin influence.

  4. Experimental
    BeyngLab, AIR V Regional Daseins / Sufi Heidegger report, working site architecture.

    The grammar, worked comparison, capture risks, and revision protocol are BeyngLab’s editable synthesis.

  5. Primary
    Janus Films, The Wind Will Carry Us, film record (Abbas Kiarostami, Iran, 1999).

    Distributor record for the crew’s purpose, the woman’s continued life, and several unseen figures. The film itself remains the primary work for close reading.

  6. Interpretive
    Kaveh Akbar, “The Wind Will Carry Us: Dust to Dust,” The Criterion Collection, May 13, 2025.

    A film-specific account of offscreen lives, labor, and the ethical risk in the documentarian’s and viewer’s gaze.

The evidence labels distinguish Corbin’s text, historical account, film records and interpretations, and BeyngLab’s experiment. They do not imply agreement among the sources.
