T9 / TOPOI

An Ereignis reading path

How does belonging happen? Ereignis.

How can we think what lets being and time belong together without turning it into another thing?

Read Ereignis across Contributions to Philosophy and Time and Being. Preserve the difference between the 1930s manuscripts and the 1962 lecture, and keep translation choices visible. “Event” is an entry into a problem, not a synonym for any dramatic occurrence.

01 / Archive trail

Two textual settings, one difficult word.

Begin with the relation itself. Then ask whether the later lecture changes the question. The translations use different vocabularies and should not be silently harmonized.

  1. 01 / Contributions to Philosophy · GA 65 · §135

    No independently existing sides

    The relation of Da-sein and beyng belongs to beyng’s own occurrence. Heidegger rejects a picture in which each first exists separately and then enters a relation. The subject–object model therefore misleads here.

    Rojcewicz–Vallega-Neu translation, p. 200
  2. 02 / Time and Being · GA 14

    Question the question “What is it?”

    The lecture questions the demand to define Appropriation as something that is. It also explicitly distinguishes its use of “event” from an ordinary occurrence. Follow the difficulty before supplying an example.

    Stambaugh translation, p. 20
  3. 03 / TRANSLATOR’S NOTE · The Event · GA 71

    See what English cannot carry at once

    The translator’s vocabulary note displays a family of terms built around eignen. It helps track the linguistic problem, but it is editorial guidance, not a stand-alone statement by Heidegger.

    Rojcewicz’s vocabulary note, p. 125

02 / Terms and distinctions

Translate without flattening.

TermDistinction to retain
EreignisRendered here through the linked editions’ “event” and “Appropriation.” Neither English word settles the argument.
AppropriationDo not assume possession by an already constituted subject. Read the relation described in Contributions before importing that model.
Seyn / beyngThe spelling signals a distinction in the Contributions translation. Preserve it when identifying the source’s vocabulary.
EnteignisZiarek brings withdrawal and disappropriation into the question of Ereignis. Treat his account as an interpretation to test in its textual setting.

03 / Readers at Ereignis

Eventfulness, withdrawal, and language.

A DOCUMENTED DISAGREEMENT

Richard Polt

“Meaning, Excess, and Event” directly engages Thomas Sheehan’s interpretation. Polt asks whether an account of conditions always already in effect gives sufficient weight to historical particularity in the 1930s writings. His claims remain explicitly sensitive to the period under discussion.

Read Polt at Ereignis
WITHDRAWAL

Krzysztof Ziarek

“Event and Nothingness” reads Ereignis through Enteignis and the nothing. Giving and withdrawal remain entangled. This challenges any account of the event as an entirely positive revelation available for us to possess.

Read Ziarek at Ereignis
BEING, TIME, AND SAYING

Theodore Kisiel

“The Language of the Event: The Event of Language” follows the linguistic problem of thinking being and time together. Read it alongside the question-and-answer difficulty on Stambaugh’s p. 20.

Read Kisiel at Ereignis

04 / Disagreement and risk

A condition, a historical opening, or a changing question?

Polt’s disagreement with Sheehan gives a concrete point of entry: how much eventfulness does Ereignis require? Ziarek and Kisiel add questions of withdrawal and saying. They should not be made into three interchangeable explanations.

Compare passages by date and work before constructing a single definition. Equally, avoid treating every disruption, innovation, or moving experience as an instance of Ereignis. The term’s difficulty demands an argument, not a grander label.

No region owns the event. Our regional grammars propose situated, revisable readings of disclosure; they do not turn histories or cultures into fixed metaphysical subjects.

05 / Reading move

Make the disagreement legible.

Experimental A comparative reading exercise.

  1. 01

    Read two passages separately.

    Summarize what each primary passage rules out. Record work, date, translator, and page beside each summary.

  2. 02

    Test a reader’s claim.

    Choose Polt’s question of historical particularity or Ziarek’s account of withdrawal. Identify what your two passages support and what requires further reading.

  3. 03

    Keep the analogy provisional.

    If you propose a contemporary encounter as an example, explain exactly what it illuminates and where it fails. Do not let its drama substitute for textual evidence.
